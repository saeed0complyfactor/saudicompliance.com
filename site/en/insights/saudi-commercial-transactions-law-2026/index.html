The Commercial Transactions Law: what fintechs should change before May 2027

Saudi Commercial Transactions Law: published 13 Nov 2026, in force 180 days later. What it means for fintech contracts, guarantees and Arabic records.

Saudi Arabia's new Commercial Transactions Law (نظام المعاملات التجارية) was published in the Umm Al-Qura gazette on 13 November 2026 and takes effect 180 days after publication, which is 12 May 2027 by our count. It replaces three old laws: the 1350H Commercial Court Law, the 1383H Commercial Papers Law and the 1409H Commercial Books Law. For fintechs, three points matter most. Banking, finance, deposit taking, money exchange, remittance, securities, insurance, brokerage and information technology are listed as commercial acts when done professionally. "Commercial books" become "accounting records", kept in Arabic under a new Ministry of Commerce regulation. And the decree approving it leaves the Saudi Central Bank's (SAMA's) powers over the firms it supervises untouched.

Key takeaways

The law was approved by Royal Decree M/132 dated 21/5/1448H. Once it takes effect, every company formed under the Companies Law is a merchant under it, whatever its purpose. Co-debtors and guarantors of a commercial debt are jointly liable unless agreed otherwise with the creditor. The detailed record-keeping rules will come in an Accounting Records Regulation, due from the Minister of Commerce within 180 days of publication. Fines for record-keeping breaches go up to SAR 50,000.

This guide is for founders, CFOs and compliance leads at payment, finance, BNPL, wealth and other fintech firms operating in Saudi Arabia. It is based on the Arabic text published in Umm Al-Qura, which is the authoritative version. The English wording of articles below is our translation. It summarises the law and does not replace advice on your own contracts.

The law sets its start as a period ("after 180 days have passed from its publication in the official gazette") and gives no calendar date. We have added 180 days to 13 November 2026. If an official Gregorian or Hijri date is announced, rely on that instead.

The law has 271 articles in nine Parts. The parts most relevant to fintechs are Part 1 (general provisions), Part 2 (commercial acts), Part 3 (the merchant), Part 5 (commercial obligations), Part 6 (accounting records), Part 7 (commercial contracts, including brokerage) and Part 8 (commercial papers: bills of exchange, promissory notes and cheques).

Under the new law, your licensed activity is a commercial act and your company a merchant

Article 6 lists acts that are commercial "for the purposes of this law and other laws related to commercial acts" when they are practised professionally. The list includes:

  • securities;
  • banking, granting finance and credit facilities, receiving deposits, money exchange and remittance services;
  • insurance of all kinds;
  • brokerage;
  • marketing services;
  • information technology.

Article 7 adds any act similar in character and purpose to the listed ones. Article 5 makes some acts commercial even when they are not done professionally, including operations on commercial papers, whoever the parties are, and telecommunications and internet services. Article 8 treats any act a merchant does for a reason connected with its business as commercial, and presumes the connection unless shown otherwise.

Article 10(c) settles who is a merchant: every company formed under the Companies Law, whatever its purpose. On our reading, a Saudi payment institution, finance company, capital market institution or fintech holding company is therefore a merchant, and its regulated business is a commercial act under the new law. Article 1 confirms the law applies whether the commercial act is carried out electronically or by traditional means.

What the law does not change for SAMA licensees

Two provisions keep the sector rules in place. Item 2 of Royal Decree M/132 says the application of the law does not affect SAMA's statutory functions and powers over the entities it supervises. Article 3 says the law does not prejudice special statutory provisions. The decree names only SAMA, so on our reading CMA and Insurance Authority rules rest on Article 3.

In practice, your licence conditions, SAMA's rules and the CMA's regulations continue to govern your regulated activity. The new law fills in the general commercial rules around it: how gaps in contracts are filled, who is liable for a debt, how records are kept and what a valid cheque or promissory note looks like. Where a sector rule speaks to the same point, check the sector rule first. For a map of who supervises what, see our guide to Saudi financial regulators.

Contracts: freedom of terms, then custom, then the Civil Transactions Law

Article 4 lets parties to a commercial act agree their own terms, unless the terms conflict with public order or a mandatory legal rule. Where there is no agreement and no express provision in the law, gaps are filled in this order:

  1. commercial custom and usage, with custom specific to a region or an activity taking priority over general custom;
  2. the Civil Transactions Law.

Article 2 matters for customer-facing fintechs. The law applies to an act that is commercial for one party and not for the other, which on our reading covers most consumer contracts with a fintech. By exception, Article 2(2) allows the parties to agree that the law does not apply to such an act. Whether to use that option in retail terms is a decision to take with counsel, alongside any consumer rules your regulator applies.

Articles 28 and 29 also add an unfair competition rule. Article 28 bars a merchant from harming its competitors, including through deceptive or misleading acts involving a competitor's products, services or business, or false claims that may undermine trust in them. Under Article 29 the harmed party can ask a court to stop the conduct, or prevent it if imminent, and claim compensation where warranted. Comparative marketing claims in app stores and campaigns should be checked against this.

Accounting records replace commercial books

From the effective date, Royal Decree item 3 replaces the phrase "commercial books" with "accounting records" wherever it appears in Saudi laws. Part 6 sets the framework:

ArticleRequirement
37Every merchant keeps an accounting system with records of its financial transactions and events and when they happened, showing its financial position, rights and obligations. The system must produce information on request at any time and support financial statements and tax and zakat returns. It may be paper or digital
38Records are kept in Arabic. Another language may be used as well, but the Arabic records prevail if they differ
39Records, their contents and supporting documents are retained as the Accounting Records Regulation sets; the documents may be kept by digital means, as that Regulation sets
40The Regulation sets minimum records for certain categories of merchant and exempt categories, taking business size into account
41Entries in mandatory records are presumed made with the merchant's knowledge and consent unless proven otherwise, and carry evidential weight
43Records must be made available to those entitled under this or other laws, or under court orders, within the period the Regulation sets
44The Ministry of Commerce may require certain categories of merchant to file an annual declaration of compliance with Part 6
46Breaches carry a fine of up to SAR 50,000; a merchant can also, or instead, be warned or ordered to take preventive or corrective steps. Fines reflect seriousness, repetition, business size and harm. Decisions can be challenged in court

Under Article 47, the Minister of Commerce forms a committee of at least three members, chaired by a legally qualified member, to hear these violations, and may list violations that can be penalised directly without going to the committee.

The Arabic-language rule is the point most likely to catch foreign-owned fintechs that run English-only ledgers on an overseas accounting platform. Article 38 allows records in another language alongside Arabic, but the Arabic records must exist and they prevail. The detail (which records are mandatory, how long to keep them, what counts as a valid digital record) waits on the Regulation, so plan for the change now and finalise the design once the Regulation is published.

Introducer fees are earned when the contract is concluded

Part 7 includes a chapter on brokerage contracts, starting at Article 102, where a broker finds a counterparty for a specific contract in return for a fee. Its default rules are relevant to referral partners, lead generators and marketplace models:

  • the broker earns its fee once the contract is concluded, even if it is not performed or its effect depends on meeting a requirement under another law (Article 104(1)); if the contract is subject to a condition precedent, no fee is due until the condition is met (Article 104(2));
  • the fee is due only for the first contract between the parties made through the broker (Article 104(3));
  • the broker does not guarantee the parties' solvency and is not liable for performance, unless there is fraud or gross error (Article 107).

If your firm pays introducers, or acts as one, check that your agreements state when a fee is earned and whether repeat business pays. Where the introduction is itself a regulated activity, the regulator's rules sit on top of these defaults.

Promissory notes need every Article 218 particular

Part 8 replaces the 1383H Commercial Papers Law. Three points stand out for lenders, BNPL providers and anyone collecting payments:

  • A promissory note must carry the particulars in Article 218: the order clause or the words "promissory note" (سند لأمر) in the body, in the language of the note; date and place of issue; an unconditional promise to pay a set sum; the payee; maturity; place of payment; and the maker's signature. Under Article 219, a document missing one of these is not a promissory note, except in the listed fallback cases for a missing place of issue, maturity or place of payment. Article 220(1) binds the maker in the same way as the acceptor of a bill of exchange, and Article 221 applies the bill of exchange rules to promissory notes where they fit. This matters to lenders such as finance companies that take notes as security.
  • Article 123 treats an interest clause in a bill of exchange as not written.
  • A cheque issued and payable in the Kingdom can be drawn only on a bank (Article 226). Article 257(3) gives SAMA the power to regulate the protest document issued by the paying bank.

Limitation periods are short. Claims on a bill of exchange against the acceptor (and so, on our reading of Articles 220 and 221, against the maker of a promissory note) are not heard after three years from maturity (Article 215). A cheque holder's claims against the drawer and endorsers are not heard after 180 days from the end of the presentation period, and against the paying bank after one year (Article 261).

The penalties for bounced cheques are in Article 263: up to three years in prison, a fine of up to SAR 50,000, or both, rising to five years and SAR 100,000 for a repeat offence within three years of a conviction. Banks face their own fines. A paying bank that falsely states there are no funds, or refuses to pay a valid funded cheque, can be fined up to SAR 100,000 (Article 264), and a bank that issues a cheque book in breach of a court ban can be fined up to SAR 500,000 (Article 266).

A business sale binds third parties only once registered

Article 17 defines the business (المتجر) to include intangible elements such as goodwill, the trade name, intellectual property, licences, lease rights, the website "and any other electronic elements". Article 18 says one intangible element can be enough for a business to exist, which fits app-only fintechs. Under Article 23, a sale or other disposal of the business or an independent branch is effective against third parties only once it is entered in the commercial register and notified to the third parties concerned.

For a regulated fintech, the transfer of a licence or a change of control also needs whatever approval the regulator's own rules require.

What to do before 12 May 2027

  1. Map references to the repealed laws. Search contracts, policies and board papers for the Commercial Court Law, the Commercial Papers Law and the Commercial Books Law, and plan updated wording that refers to the new law and to "accounting records".
  2. Review default liability clauses. Decide, contract by contract, whether you want the Article 30 joint liability default for co-borrowers and guarantors, and say so expressly where you do not.
  3. Check retail terms against Article 2. With counsel, decide whether your customer terms should rely on the new law or use the Article 2(2) option, and record the decision.
  4. Prepare Arabic accounting records. If your ledger runs only in English or offshore, scope how you will produce Arabic records that can be made available on request. Finalise the design once the Accounting Records Regulation is published.
  5. Refresh promissory note and cheque templates. Make sure every note template carries all the Article 218 particulars and that collections teams know the Article 215 and 261 time limits.
  6. Update introducer and referral agreements. State when a fee is earned and whether repeat business pays, so the Article 104 defaults do not decide it for you.
  7. Watch for the Regulation. The Accounting Records Regulation is due within 180 days of 13 November 2026. We check Umm Al-Qura daily and will update this guide when it appears.
