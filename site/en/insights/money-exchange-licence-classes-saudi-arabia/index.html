Class A, B or C: choosing a money changer licence in Saudi Arabia

SAMA's register sorts 64 money changers into Classes A, B and C. What each class allows, the capital it needs, and why new applicants choose B or C.

Saudi Compliance editorial teamPublished: 2026-11-24Updated: 2026-11-2411 min read

A new money changer in Saudi Arabia can be licensed for the activities SAMA's register labels Class C, buying, selling and trading foreign currencies inside the Kingdom, or Class B, which adds the import and export of currencies. Class A, which also covers money transfers inside and outside the Kingdom, is held by one firm and is closed to new applicants, because the 2020 Rules limit transfers to firms already licensed for them. As of 24 November 2026, SAMA's register lists 64 money changers: 1 in Class A, 11 in Class B and 52 in Class C.

Key takeaways

The class letters come from SAMA's register, and each matches a set of activities in Article 11 of the Rules Regulating Money Changing Business. Class C needs SAR 2 million of paid-up capital and Class B SAR 7 million. Class B is open only to companies and branches of foreign money changers, so a sole proprietorship can hold Class C only. On SAMA's register, all 12 firms whose names are marked "Est." are in Class C. No class lets you hold customer accounts, take deposits or lend.

What each class covers

SAMA's public list of licensed money exchange centres labels every licensee with a class and a short list of activities. The Rules themselves do not use the word "class"; they list the permitted activities in Article 11, which also lets SAMA designate other activities from time to time. The table puts the register labels and the Rules side by side.

The capital floors apply to money changers established in the Kingdom; for branches of foreign money changers, SAMA sets the invested capital (Art 7). Article 9 sets issue and renewal fees by licence scope (currency dealing alone, or one or more Article 11 activities) and a renewal fee for transfer licences; the amounts are listed on our money exchange licence page.

The Rules are No. 4686 dated 11 July 2020 (21/11/1441H), and the SAMA Rulebook shows them as in force when we read them on 24 November 2026. They replaced the money changing rules issued by Minister of Finance Decision No. 1357 of 01/05/1432H. The Arabic text, القواعد المنظمة لمزاولة أعمال الصرافة, matches the English on the points in this guide with two exceptions: the Arabic names the partnership form in Article 6 as a general partnership, and it adds a phrase to Article 11 noted under Class A. Where they differ, the Arabic prevails. The register's Arabic labels are فئة (أ)، فئة (ب) and فئة (ج), and each licence number carries the letter: 040 - أ/ص, for example, is a Class A number.

SAMA said that licence brought the number of licensed money changers to 64.
  • The five highest licence numbers, 136 to 140, are two Class B firms (Altabdil Finance Company and Trafileks) and three Class C firms (Shurayh Abdulrahman Albayhani Co, Bawabat Al-Umulat Exchange Co. and Global Exchange AS Currency Service Limited). The register shows no grant dates, so we do not read the numbers as dates.
  • On 9 November 2026 SAMA revoked the licence of Ahmed Ibrahim Suleiman Al-Buti Establishment for Money Exchange under paragraph (b/7) of Article 34. SAMA's Arabic notice gives the licence number, 122-ج/ ص, a Class C number. That paragraph lets SAMA end a licence on the death of the owner of a money changer that is a sole proprietorship. Licence 122 is no longer on the list.
  • SAMA's 13 October statement gave 64, and we count 64 entries on the 24 November register, although licence 122 was revoked in between. The register shows no grant dates, so we cannot say which entry accounts for the difference.

Class C: currency dealing, open to every legal form

Class C is the entry route. Article 6 accepts five legal forms: a joint stock company, a limited liability company, a general partnership (شركة تضامن in the Arabic, which prevails; the English says "partnership"), a sole proprietorship, or a branch of a foreign company licensed for money changing. All five can hold the currency dealing activity in Article 11(a).

The paid-up capital floor is SAR 2 million. The application must also carry an irrevocable bank guarantee in SAMA's favour equal to the capital, which SAMA releases when it decides the application. Plan funding for the capital and the guarantee until that decision.

Legal form matters after licensing too. Under Article 23(a), SAMA's non-objection to open a branch is limited to money changers that are companies or branches of a foreign money changer. A sole proprietorship can therefore run only the location it is licensed for. Article 34 adds sole-proprietor risks: SAMA may end the licence at the owner's request or on the owner's death, which is the ground it used on 9 November 2026.

Class B: adding cash import and export

Class B adds Article 11(b), the import and export of currencies. The Rules put two conditions on it. The money changer must be a company or a branch of a foreign money changer, and it must also hold the currency dealing licence. A sole proprietorship cannot reach Class B, which fits the register: every Class B name is a company.

The capital floor rises to SAR 7 million for money changers licensed to import and export cash, and the bank guarantee rises with it. Article 18 then requires policies and procedures for cash movements, including notifying customs officers at border crossings of the amounts imported and exported and keeping the import and export documents. Firms that expect wholesale banknote or travel-currency flows across borders should price Class B from the start; moving up later means raising paid-up capital to SAR 7 million, and Article 23(d) requires SAMA's prior non-objection to any change in the structure of the capital.

Class A: closed to new applicants

The closing paragraph of Article 11 permits money transfers inside and outside the Kingdom only for holders of valid SAMA transfer licences at the time the Rules were issued, and it bars those holders from opening new branches for transfers. The Arabic adds that the bar covers branches other than the existing ones (خلاف القائمة). Class A therefore cannot grow by new licences, and on 24 November 2026 it had one member.

The Rules still carry the Class A numbers: SAR 10 million of paid-up capital, a cash reserve of at least 10% of capital against 5% for other money changers, and a SAR 40,000 renewal fee. If your product moves money between people from a payment account, look at a payment or e-money licence: transfers from a payment account are payment services under SAMA's Payments Implementing Regulations. Article 7(17) of those Regulations excludes "money remittance", where funds are received without a payment account being created for the payer or payee, and we found no standalone remittance licence for new entrants; confirm the route with SAMA for that model. Our comparison of e-money and payment institution licences sets out which category fits which flow, and the payment institution licence page covers the application.

What no class allows

Article 12 lists what every money changer is prohibited from doing, whatever its class. The items most relevant to fintech founders are:

  • opening current, investment, savings or any other accounts for customers or employees
  • accepting deposits or trusts in any form, in cash or in kind
  • extending credit, managing a loan or pledging its assets as collateral
  • issuing letters of guarantee or opening letters of credit
  • speculating in foreign currencies, precious metals, stocks or commodities

Article 19 adds that a money changer needs SAMA's non-objection before providing e-services, launching a new product or changing an existing one. On our reading of Article 12, a money changer cannot run a currency app that keeps customer balances. The Payments Implementing Regulations exclude money exchange under the Money Changing Rules only where funds are not held on a payment account, so a stored balance points to the payments regime, often an e-money licence. Check your flow against both rulebooks before you choose.

Do you need a licence at all?

Article 4(a) prohibits money changing in the Kingdom without a SAMA licence, and it also bars unlicensed firms from using "money changer", "currency exchanger" or similar terms in any language in their name, papers or advertising. Article 4(b) carves out a narrow exception. Licensed hotels, hotel apartments and travel agencies may exchange currencies for their own customers only, provided they sell the currencies on to a licensed bank or money changer, comply with the Anti-Money Laundering Law and its Implementing Regulations, and document and archive the transactions. A platform serving the public at large does not fit that exception.

From approval to opening day

Two deadlines in the Rules can cost you the approval or the licence, and a third governs renewal.

  1. Apply to SAMA with a feasibility study covering the business plan and administrative structure, the bank guarantee and, for companies, the memorandum and articles of association and the proposed ownership structure (Art 6).
  2. Once procedures and the head office are ready, SAMA writes to the Ministry of Commerce; SAMA issues the licence after the commercial register is issued (Art 6, part III).
  3. If you do not complete the procedures and obtain the licence within six months of SAMA's approval, the approval is cancelled, unless SAMA extends the period (Art 10(a)).
  4. If you do not start business within three months of the licence date, the licence is cancelled, unless SAMA extends the period (Art 10(b)). Fees are not refunded if a licence is revoked (Art 10(c)).
  5. The licence lasts up to five years. File the renewal application at least six months before expiry (Art 8).

Article 21 also requires compliance with SAMA's Saudization, employment-company and senior positions instructions, and the appointment of a qualified compliance officer. Build those hires into the plan before you file; our compliance function page covers the roles.

How to choose between B and C

  1. Decide whether you will ever move physical cash across the border. If yes, you need Class B, a company structure and SAR 7 million of paid-up capital.
  2. If you plan more than one location, use a company form even for Class C, because Article 23(a) limits branch approvals to companies and foreign branches.
  3. If customers will hold balances, send money to other people or receive credit, stop and test the product against the payments regime before you apply as a money changer.
  4. Before partnering with an existing money changer, check its class and licence number on SAMA's list; a Class C firm cannot lawfully run your cross-border cash leg.
